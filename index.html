Thesis 2

  1. Actuators are not solved, and they are where hardware value sits.
    • Actuators are 40 to 60% of a humanoid's bill of materials and the main driver of its cost and performance.
    • Actuators sit at every joint, one per degree of freedom, in a physical AI system. Every step, grasp and turn is an actuator moving. Tesla Optimus, for example, has 28 actuated joints plus about 50 more actuators in its Gen 3 hands.
    • A real technology breakthrough here would be a game changer. Most actuator motors rely on rare-earth permanent magnets, and China controls about 69% of rare-earth mining and 90% of magnet processing. Any innovation that removes the rare-earth dependency would matter globally.
    Sources: McKinsey, "Turning humanoid supply chain constraints into billion-dollar wins", Apr 2026 · Humanoid.guide Actuation Report, Aug 2026

Thesis 3

  1. Robot brains will be won by a few capital-heavy labs, not by Indian venture.
    • The winning stack is a VLM that plans, a VLA that acts, and world models that generate the training data. Google's Gemini Robotics 1.5 already ships this split, and skills learned on one robot transferred to a humanoid without retraining.
    • Every layer is compute- and cost-heavy. Frontier training cost has grown about 2.4x a year, and even a 2B-parameter robot model took about 50,000 H100 GPU hours.
    • Pure-play labs are priced far beyond an Indian VC cheque: Skild AI raised $1.4B at $14B in January 2026, and Physical Intelligence is raising about $1B at over $11B.
    • Training these models is not an Indian venture play. The opening is downstream: fine-tuning an open model like π0 to a new task takes only 1 to 20 hours of data.
    Sources: Epoch AI · NVIDIA GR00T N1 paper · TechCrunch · Google DeepMind · Pulse 2.0 · Dealroom, Mar 2026 · Physical Intelligence

Thesis 4

  1. Sensing, locomotion, manipulation & dexterity can give venture outcomes.
    • Sensing. Cameras are solved; the other senses are not. Infrared vision still costs thousands of dollars per chip, and touch, force, sound and smell are early for robots. Making any of these cheap is a business: TriEye claims infrared on silicon at 1,000x lower cost, and Xense already sells tactile sensors to Agibot and Google.
    • Locomotion. A general-purpose humanoid is overkill for most jobs. Indian shop floors and workflows look nothing like Western factories, which gives purpose-built mobile robots (AMRs) the right to win across India and other developing markets, a very large opportunity.
    • Manipulation. General-purpose manipulation is not solved: Physical Intelligence's model folds t-shirts 95% of the time but jeans only 75%. Closing that gap is critical for humanoids. Solving manipulation for one specialised use case is also a large market: it sits at the application layer and won't carry over to every task, but it lets companies sell turnkey solutions to industry after industry.
    • Dexterity is the biggest prize. Tesla halted Optimus production in 2025 because of its hands, and Unitree's own IPO filing says its hands are not precise or durable enough for sustained use. Whoever solves the hand unlocks general-purpose robots.
    Sources: Yole Group · eeNews Europe · KrASIA · Hardware Busters, Aug 2026 · Epoch AI, 2026 · TechSpot

Thesis 5

  1. Robot data is a business, and it should be judged on three dimensions.
    • Modality. Does the data go beyond video? Force, touch, sound and vision across the spectrum (not just visible light), and audio across frequencies, are scarce and worth more.
    • Fit with the robot. Data cannot be sold in isolation; it must match the body, sensors and skills of the robot being trained. Videos of people tightening bolts will not teach a system built to drive. With far more dimensions than text, physical AI needs much more data in total than LLMs do.
    • Long tail. Value rises with how niche the action is. Folding a t-shirt barely changes with its colour; driving changes with the soil and road surface, so edge cases are where data earns its price.

Life cycle of robot data

01 · Pretraining

Bulk egocentric video teaches the basics. It commoditises fast, and prices are already falling.

02 · Fine-tuning

Models then need site-, robot- and task-specific data, including force and touch, collected by skilled domain workers.

03 · Fleet data

Once deployed, failure and recovery data from working fleets matters most. It sits with fleet operators and incumbents, and is far more valuable.

01Sorting Problem in a Warehouse
Problem
  • Today's warehouse sorting systems scale linearly. To double the speed, you have to install a second sorting system of the same size.

Current systems

Proposed system

Scalability · time to sort a day's parcels

Loop and tree: serial

Every parcel rides one belt, one behind the other. Throughput is fixed at belt speed ÷ carrier spacing, about 16,500 an hour per loop. To go faster you build a second full system, so capacity grows in whole-system steps: linear.

Okura SCB-15: 2.3 m/s ÷ 0.5 m carrier pitch = 4.6 parcels/s
4.6 × 3,600 s = 16,560/hr (Okura's rated max: 16,500)

Grid: parallel

Parcels cross the grid in several lanes at once. Throughput = lanes × rate per lane, so capacity grows by adding lanes on the same grid, not whole systems.

One lane ≈ 5,200/hr (Cellumation's omni-wheel module)
Lanes that fit side by side: a parcel covers 7 wheels + 1 wheel of clearance = 8 wheels, so a 100-wheel-wide grid fits 100 ÷ 8 = 12 lanes
12 × 5,200 = 62,400/hr at most, if lanes never block each other

Parcels per day2.3MDelhivery average day · 7.2M on its Oct-25 peak day
One loop sorter139 hrsfor 2.3M parcels · 7 loops to finish in 20 hrs · 22 at peak
One omni-wheel grid≥37 hrsfor 2.3M parcels · 2 grids to finish in 20 hrs · 6 at peak (best case)
Approach
  1. Start with daily volume: 2.3M parcels on an average day and 7.2M at peak, sorted in a 20-hour window. That needs 115,000/hr on average and 360,000/hr at peak.
  2. Loop: 2.3M ÷ 16,500 = 139 hours for one loop. 115,000 ÷ 16,500 = 7 loops; at peak, 22.
  3. Grid: 2.3M ÷ 62,400 = 37 hours for one grid at best. 115,000 ÷ 62,400 = 2 grids; at peak, 6. If lanes block each other half the time, double these.
  4. What matters: each extra loop is a full belt, induction line and chute set. The grid adds capacity a lane at a time (~5,200/hr), so it can grow in step with volume.
Inputs
Daily parcels2.3M avg · 7.2M peakS Delhivery Oct-25, whole network
Loop speed · pitch2.3 m/s · 0.5 mS Okura SCB-15 spec
Loop rate16,500/hrS Okura; Interroll quotes up to 20,000
Rate per grid lane5,200/hrS Cellumation celluveyor module
Lanes per grid12A from our model's geometry, untested
Lane blockingnone (best case)A the number to prove in a pilot
Sort window20 hrs/dayA two shifts plus changeover

A loop's speed is capped by one belt, so doubling volume means doubling the system. A grid's speed is set by how many lanes run in parallel, so it grows a lane at a time. The open question is how much lanes slow each other down.

Sources: ITLN · Delhivery Oct 2025 · Okura SCB · Interroll · Cellumation celluveyor

02AMRs Needed in Textile Factories
Problem
  • In a textile factory with about 80 workers per shift, roughly 17 (over 20%) do nothing but pick and drop yarn, thread, raw material and finished goods between stations. That makes it a strong target for automation.

Payback · business view

What is being solved. 17 of 80 workers on each shift only move material between stations, and absenteeism of about 10% a day leaves lines short of material. AMRs take over the moves, so material arrives on time whoever shows up.

Today₹50 lakha year on the material moves AMRs can take over (2 shifts)
Imported-price AMR4.7 yrspayback at ₹15 lakh a robot, 2 shifts
Built-for-India AMR2.2 yrspayback at ₹8 lakh a robot (1.4 yrs on 3 shifts)
Approach
  1. Not every move can be automated. AMRs take the station-to-station runs, about 12 of the 17 handlers per shift. Loading at stations stays manual.
  2. Labour freed = 12 × 2 shifts × ₹17,500 loaded × 12 months = ₹50.4 lakh a year.
  3. Fleet size. One AMR covers about 1.2 handlers per shift (a vendor case: 3 AMRs replaced 7 operators over 2 shifts), so the factory needs 10 AMRs, plus ₹15 lakh for integration.
  4. At ₹15 lakh a robot: capex ₹1.65 cr and maintenance at 10% is ₹15 lakh a year, so payback takes 4.7 years. Most owners will not buy at that.
  5. Work back to the price that sells. A 2-year payback on 2 shifts needs about ₹8 lakh a robot. That is roughly one-third of an imported unit, which is what Indian makers such as Hachidori say they already reach.

At Indian wages, an imported-spec AMR does not pay back in a garment unit. The opportunity is a simple, rugged AMR at about ₹8 lakh, sold first to 3-shift spinning mills, where it pays back in under 1.5 years. Fewer line stoppages from absenteeism are an extra gain not counted here.

03Dark Warehouse Needed in the Middle of the City
Problem
  • Warehouses are single-storey sheds built for people, so they sit on cheap land at the city edge and the last mile is long.

Proposed system

The dark tower warehouse

Remove the people and the format changes: storage becomes a dense cube that grows up, not out, and moves next to the customer.

  • ≈ 60 m18 storage levels
  • ~7,300 m²urban plot
  • 0people on site

Payback · business view

What is being solved. An order shipped from a city-edge warehouse passes through manual picking, a sort centre, a line-haul truck and an in-city delivery hub before a rider takes it out. That costs about ₹97 an order. An automated tower inside the city removes the line-haul and the delivery hub, automates picking, and shortens each rider's run.

City-edge shed₹97per order, fulfilment plus logistics
Dark tower₹45per order, before capex
Capex it can afford₹570 crfor a 3-year payback at 1 lakh orders a day
Approach
  1. Break today's cost per order into stages: fulfilment ₹22 + sort centre ₹10 + line-haul and in-city hub ₹21 + last mile ₹44 (rider ₹35 plus hub) = ₹97.
  2. Re-cost each stage in the tower. Automated picking: ₹3 (labour −85%). In-building grid sort: ₹2. Bulk truck replenishment: ₹5. Rider with a short run: ₹30. Energy, maintenance and remote ops: ₹5. Total ₹45.
  3. The saving is ₹52 per order. At 1 lakh orders a day (about 50 quick-commerce dark stores' worth), that is ₹190 cr a year.
  4. Instead of guessing the tower's price, work out the most it can cost and still pay back in 3 years: 3 × ₹190 cr ≈ ₹570 cr, all-in for land, structure and automation.
  5. Land check. The tower holds ~3 lakh m³ of usable storage on a 7,300 m² plot. A shed (22% of its cube usable, 12 m high) would need ~1.16 lakh m², or about 16x the land. That is why in-city land at a 1.5–2.5x rent premium is affordable.

India Thesis

Why India can play

  1. Talent. India can staff a robot company at a fraction of US cost. A robotics engineer earns ₹2–10 lakh a year here, against a ~$149K base salary in the US, and Mercedes-Benz and ABB already run some of their largest R&D centres in India.
  2. Demand is untapped. India has 30 robots per 10,000 factory workers, against 132 worldwide and 1,220 in Korea. In India, 45% of new robot installs (2024) go to the automotive sector alone.
  3. Policy tailwind. PLI schemes pay companies to manufacture in India, with ₹2.16 lakh cr of committed investment across 14 sectors. Every new production line widens the scope for automation across sectors.
  4. Western systems don't fit. Indian sites are more complex and less structured, so solutions built for the West rarely retrofit directly. India needs systems that are adapted heavily or built from the ground up.

Where India plays

  • Strongest: purpose-built robots for Indian shop floors and logistics (L5).
  • Strong: actuators and motors built on the EV supply chain (L1).
  • Selective: robot data capture (L3), and autonomy tooling only after revenue (L4).
  • No: foundation models (L2), which need capital only global labs have.

What works against it

  • Thin capital. Indian robotics startups raised $42M in H1 2026, and deeptech gets ~$1B a year.
  • Cheap labour. The average factory wage is ~₹2.28 lakh a year, so robots must win on throughput, space or reliability, not on wages alone.
  • Imported parts. Over 80% of magnets come from China, and the national robotics strategy is still a 2023 draft.
